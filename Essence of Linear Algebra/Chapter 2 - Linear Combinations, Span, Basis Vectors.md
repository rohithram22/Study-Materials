

When you think the coordinates as scalers, the ***basis*** vectors are the one the actually scale.

Two basis vectors can be given scalar values and adding them could help reach any possible two dimensional vector.

Anytime you are scaling vector and adding them it is called a **Linear Combination.**

Linear combination of $$\vec v\;and\;\vec w$$
$$ a\vec v + b\vec w $$

where a and b are scalers.

The **span** of two vectors v and w is the set of all their linear combinations (set of combinations formed with different values for scalers). The span of two vectors is a way of asking what are all possible vectors that we could reach with the two fundamental operations - vector addition and scalar multiplication.

One way of representing of a collection of vector is as points (since all the vectors in a coordinate system will make it crowded) with the tip represented as the point and tail to be assumed as the origin.

If thinking of vector as a own - think of it as an arrow, otherwise if its a span think of it as a point.

What happens when there is a 3rd vector -

1. If 3rd vector is sitting on the span of the first 2 then the span does not change (trapped on the flat sheet)
2. If you randomly choose a third vector - it moves around the span sheet of the first two sweeping it around space (imagine the sheet moving up and down in the image) -accesses the full 3 dimensional space


When you have multiple vectors and you could remove one without affecting the span, they are called **linearly dependent**. One of the vectors can be expressed as the linear combination of the others. 
![[Pasted image 20250810200742.png]]
(image - linearly dependent)
If each vector adds another dimension to the span they are said to be **linearly independent**.


The technical definition of basis in a space:

The **basis** of a vector space is a set of **linearly independent** vectors that **span** the full space.
